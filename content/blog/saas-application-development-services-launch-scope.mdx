SaaS application development services help teams build software that customers access as an ongoing service. The scope extends beyond interface development: customers need onboarding, appropriate access, reliable workflows, and support as the product changes. Where subscriptions are involved, billing events and product permissions also need to work together.

This guide helps UK and US founders and product teams prepare a launch brief. It focuses on the responsibilities specific to operating a shared software product, rather than repeating a general feature list. ## Define the customer outcome before the subscription

Describe who will use the product and what useful task they will complete. A subscription model does not explain why a customer would return. Identify the recurring problem, the current workaround, and the point at which the application delivers value.

For example, a fictional supplier compliance product might let procurement teams request documents, review them, and track upcoming expiry dates. Its first useful outcome is a reviewed supplier record with a clear status. A dashboard full of charts would not replace that workflow. This example illustrates planning and is not a Shardwise client result.

Write one end-to-end scenario for the first release. Include who provides information, who makes decisions, what the customer sees, and how mistakes are corrected. List assumptions that still need customer research. Development can implement a defined workflow, but it cannot prove product demand simply by shipping it.

## Treat onboarding as part of the product

Decide how a new customer becomes ready to use the application. They may create a workspace, invite colleagues, import records, and configure notifications. Each step should have an owner and an understandable completion condition.

Avoid requiring users to finish extensive configuration before experiencing the core workflow unless the product truly needs it. Ask which information is essential immediately and which can be added later. Explain incomplete states so customers do not mistake missing setup for a broken product.

For the compliance example, a team might first add one supplier and request one document. Bulk import and custom templates could follow. Include invitation expiry, duplicate accounts, and incomplete imports in the scope, rather than considering onboarding finished when a registration form works.

Choose evidence that shows whether onboarding succeeds: customers completing the first useful task, common support requests, and steps where users stop. Agree what data is appropriate to collect and how your team will review it.

## Specify tenant boundaries explicitly

A tenant is a customer account or organisation whose resources must be separated from those of other customers. If several organisations use the product, define what belongs to each tenant: records, uploads, reports, settings, and background jobs.

AWS's [tenant isolation guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/saas-architecture-fundamentals/tenant-isolation.html) distinguishes tenant isolation from ordinary sign-in and permission checks. A user being authenticated does not, on its own, establish that they can only access their organisation's resources. Ask the delivery team how tenant context is enforced across the application.

Discuss shared and dedicated infrastructure as options, not as automatic answers. Requirements, operating costs, and customer commitments should guide the choice. Request a clear explanation of how the chosen approach prevents cross-customer access.

Include isolation tests for search results, exports, file downloads, cached responses, and background processing. A correctly filtered dashboard is insufficient if an export or attachment link exposes another customer's information. Document support access as well: staff should understand what they can view and how exceptional access is recorded.

## Separate roles from paid feature access

Role permissions answer what a user may do within an organisation. Subscription entitlements answer what the customer's plan includes. An administrator may have permission to manage reports while their organisation's plan does not include an advanced reporting feature.

Define both sets of rules. List the actions available to members, administrators, reviewers, and support staff where relevant. Then specify any plan limits, such as available features, seats, or usage allowances. Describe how these rules interact.

Plan changes before implementing them. What happens when a customer downgrades below their current seat count? Can they still read existing records? Does cancellation take effect immediately or at the end of a paid period? These are product policies your team must decide, not behaviours to leave to accidental implementation.

Keep checks consistent at the point where operations are processed. Hiding a premium button in the interface does not prevent a direct request to the underlying operation.

## Model billing as an ongoing lifecycle

If the product charges subscriptions, scope more than the initial checkout. Include trials if offered, renewals, payment failures, plan changes, cancellation, and restoration of access. Define what customers and support staff see at each stage.

Stripe's [subscription webhook documentation](https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/webhooks) explains how integrations receive asynchronous subscription and payment notifications. If Stripe is your provider, map the relevant events to your access policies and test the integration. Other providers need their own reviewed event model.

Do not treat a customer returning to a success page as the sole proof of their subscription status. Agree how the application verifies events, handles repeated notifications, and reconciles its records with the billing provider. Describe recovery when an event cannot be processed.

For example, a failed renewal might trigger a customer notification and a defined grace period rather than immediate deletion of their records. The right policy depends on the product. Record that decision in the brief so development, customer messaging, and support follow the same rules.

## Plan integrations and background work

Many SaaS products send messages, import files, synchronise external records, or generate reports. Specify which tasks happen during an interactive request and which continue in the background. Users should know when work is pending, complete, or unsuccessful.

For the supplier example, a bulk import could produce a summary of accepted rows and errors. Retrying the failed rows should not create duplicate suppliers. Define how progress is displayed, which records are changed, and whether a partial import can be reversed.

List external dependencies, account ownership, test access, usage limits, and recurring costs. Ask how a provider outage affects the core workflow. If notifications fail, can customers still see the task in the application? If an integration is unavailable, what can staff safely retry?

Carry tenant context into background jobs and file processing. Work performed after the browser request ends still needs the correct customer boundaries and permissions.

## Test the lifecycle as well as individual screens

A SaaS acceptance plan should cover the journey from onboarding through routine use and eventual cancellation or data export. Test different roles, different tenants, and relevant subscription states rather than only one happy-path account.

Useful scenarios include an expired invitation, a user removed from a workspace, a repeated billing event, an interrupted import, and a request for another tenant's file. Define expected results so the business team can review demonstrations against observable behaviour.

Combine automated checks for important rules and integrations with manual review of usability, keyboard access, mobile layouts, and error messages. Use realistic test data without exposing confidential customer information. Include larger datasets where they could affect search, reports, or exports.

The [OWASP Application Security Verification Standard](https://owasp.org/projects/asvs) can help identify security requirements and verification work. Select appropriate controls with the delivery team and record evidence. Mentioning a standard does not establish compliance or replace a product-specific review.

## Make operations part of the launch brief

Agree who handles deployment, monitoring, support, and recovery. A shared product needs a way to identify failures affecting one customer as well as incidents affecting everyone. Ask which logs and alerts help the team understand an issue without unnecessarily exposing sensitive information.

Plan backups and restoration with the chosen data model. Discuss whether restoring one customer's records is possible and how recovery affects changes made after the backup. Document the limits rather than assuming every recovery request is simple.

Define release approval, rollback or recovery steps, and customer communication responsibilities. Changes to data structures may need a different recovery approach from a change to interface text. The team should explain those differences before launch.

Account ownership and handover matter here too. Confirm access to the repository, hosting, billing provider, messaging services, and monitoring tools. Request setup instructions and a support runbook so operation does not depend entirely on one person's memory.

## Compare service proposals using a complete first release

Ask providers of SaaS application development services to separate discovery, product design, implementation, integration, testing, launch, and ongoing support. Compare assumptions and acceptance criteria alongside the quoted amount.

Prioritise a complete core workflow with adequate isolation, access rules, and operating support. Additional analytics, flexible plan builders, and extensive integrations may be later work. Record exclusions so stakeholders understand what the first release actually contains.

Separate development costs from ongoing hosting, storage, email, billing fees, monitoring, and maintenance. Avoid universal budget or timeline assumptions: customer boundaries, data migration, integration reliability, and product policies can change the work considerably.

## SaaS development planning questions

### Does every SaaS application need subscriptions?

No. Software delivered as a service may use different commercial arrangements. If subscriptions are part of your model, define billing and access behaviour explicitly. Do not add plan complexity before understanding how customers will use and purchase the product.

### Is sign-in enough to separate customer data?

No. The application also needs tenant boundaries applied to relevant resources and operations. Ask for evidence covering queries, exports, files, and background jobs, as well as the visible interface.

### What belongs in a first SaaS release?

Include a useful customer workflow, onboarding needed to reach it, appropriate access and isolation, relevant billing behaviour, and operational support. Defer additional features where doing so does not compromise those essentials.

### Should we build our own billing system?

Evaluate established providers before taking on payment infrastructure yourself. Even with a provider, your application must handle the relationship between subscription state and product access. Compare supported markets, integration requirements, and operating responsibilities for your project.

### What should happen when a customer leaves?

Define cancellation, access changes, export, retention, and deletion policies. Communicate those policies clearly and implement them consistently. Involve the appropriate specialist when contracts or regulatory requirements affect the decisions.

## Prepare the SaaS brief before choosing the stack

Start with the customer workflow, then define tenant boundaries, roles, subscription policies where relevant, integrations, and operating responsibilities. A clear brief helps you evaluate SaaS application development services against the product you actually need to launch.
