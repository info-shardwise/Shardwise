Website redesign services help a business review an existing site and improve its content, navigation, interface, or implementation. A useful redesign starts by identifying the customer tasks that need improvement and the pages, information, and systems worth retaining. The scope should include verification before and after launch, not just new visual layouts.

This guide helps UK and US business owners compare a focused refresh with a larger redesign. It explains what evidence to collect, which deliverables to request, and how to review the release. ## Decide whether the problem needs a redesign

Write down the symptoms first. Customers may struggle to understand a service, the mobile menu may hide essential links, or editors may find routine updates difficult. A website can also look dated while still serving its customers effectively. Different problems need different responses.

For a fictional consultancy, a low-quality enquiry might result from unclear service descriptions rather than the colour palette. A broken contact form needs diagnosis and repair even if a redesign is planned. These examples illustrate decisions; they are not Shardwise client results.

Ask a provider to distinguish design, content, and technical issues. Request evidence for its recommendation and identify the smallest coherent change that addresses the problem. Avoid approving a full rebuild solely because it is the most prominent option in a proposal.

## Record the current website before changing it

Collect an inventory of important pages, forms, integrations, and editing tasks. Where data is available, review landing pages, search queries, and completed enquiries. Record the date range and how those figures were measured so later comparisons have context.

Also capture qualitative evidence: customer questions, support issues, editor difficulties, and examples of broken journeys. Note whether a problem affects one page or a shared component. A navigation defect can affect the entire site, while outdated information may need a content update.

Use that inventory to identify what should be retained, improved, merged, or removed. A page with little traffic might still be essential for existing customers. A page with many visits may need careful content review before it is shortened or replaced. Traffic alone should not determine every content decision.

## Agree the deliverables in the proposal

A redesign proposal should connect the review to specific outputs. Depending on scope, ask for:

- Findings and priorities from the current-site review.
- A page inventory, navigation plan, and content responsibilities.
- Designs for representative page types and interaction states.
- An implementation plan and integration responsibilities.
- Content migration and URL-change decisions where relevant.
- A test plan, launch checklist, and acceptance process.
- Editing guidance, account handover, and ongoing support arrangements.

Clarify exclusions. Copywriting, photography, translations, product data, and third-party subscriptions may be separate work. A quote that includes only visual design is different from one that includes implementation and deployment.

Ask who will review each milestone and what input is needed. Compare proposals against the same scope rather than assuming the label “website redesign services” defines every deliverable.

## Plan content changes with real examples

Review whether the existing copy answers the customer's questions. Identify missing service details, outdated claims, and information repeated inconsistently across pages. Prepare representative content before approving layouts so long headings and detailed descriptions are tested early.

Keep useful information even when its presentation changes. If a service page already explains important limitations and next steps, preserve that purpose rather than replacing it with a short generic introduction. Update evidence and claims accurately; do not introduce new case studies or results merely to fill the design.

Agree how editors will publish future changes. Ask for a demonstration using a realistic task, such as adding a case study or updating a service. Our [small-business website design guide](/blogs/small-business-website-design-essential-pages/) can help connect page content with customer decisions.

## Review URL and search changes before release

A visual redesign does not necessarily require different URLs. Retain meaningful addresses where they still fit the content. When addresses change, map important old URLs to relevant new destinations and test the resulting redirects.

[Google's site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) covers migrations with URL changes, including mapping URLs, redirects, and monitoring. It also explains that search visibility can fluctuate while a move is processed. No redesign process can promise that rankings will remain unchanged.

Review titles, descriptions, canonical URLs, internal links, and indexing settings in the actual release output. Confirm that any preview-only indexing restrictions are handled appropriately for production. Avoid redirecting unrelated old pages to the homepage just to remove errors.

Our [website migration SEO checklist](/blogs/website-migration-seo-checklist/) provides a deeper release review. Discuss [technical and on-page SEO](/services/technical-onpage-seo/) when the change includes substantial navigation, content, or implementation work.

## Test the redesigned customer journeys

Select important tasks and repeat them across representative page types. For a service site, test finding the right offer, opening its enquiry route, receiving validation feedback, and confirming delivery to the intended destination. For an online shop, use suitable test orders and inspect payment and fulfilment behavior.

Review narrow layouts, keyboard navigation, enlarged text, and important loading or error states. [W3C's accessibility evaluation overview](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/) explains evaluation approaches and the value of integrating review into development. An automated score alone is not a comprehensive accessibility assessment.

Compare performance using consistent pages and conditions. Our [Core Web Vitals checklist](/blogs/core-web-vitals-checklist-small-business/) helps review loading, responsiveness, and visual stability. A faster homepage does not establish that a heavier service page or checkout works equally well.

Record expected and actual results, unresolved issues, and who accepts the release. Test with representative content rather than relying only on short placeholders.

## Prepare the launch and recovery responsibilities

Agree who authorises deployment, updates domain settings if needed, and performs the production checks. Confirm the backup and recovery approach appropriate to the platform. If new orders or submissions continue during the change, identify how current data will be preserved.

Define a recovery decision: which failures would require restoring the previous version, who can do it, and what happens to data created after the release? A static website and a transactional store need different recovery plans.

Before approving handover, collect source and design access, deployment instructions, an account inventory, known issues, and editing guidance. Keep credentials in appropriate access controls rather than a general document. Confirm responsibility for domains, subscriptions, and maintenance.

## Review the result after launch

Check the public site after release even when the preview passed. Verify critical pages, redirects, enquiry delivery, purchase flows where relevant, and measurement configuration. Review errors and reported issues before assuming an unexpected drop is simply a search-engine adjustment.

Compare enquiries and traffic against the baseline with context. Different campaigns, seasonality, changed tracking, and a small sample can affect the comparison. Evaluate useful customer tasks and qualified enquiries alongside search visibility.

Assign continuing checks through the [website maintenance checklist](/blogs/website-maintenance-checklist-small-business/) or an agreed [maintenance service](/services/website-maintenance/). A redesign is a release point in the website's life, followed by content and operational work.

## Questions to ask before approving a redesign

1. Which observed problems justify the proposed scope?
2. Which pages, content, and URLs will be retained?
3. Who supplies content and approves its accuracy?
4. Which integrations and editing tasks are included?
5. How will testing, deployment, and recovery be verified?
6. Which accounts and assets will be handed over?
7. What checks and support are included after launch?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a website redesign mean changing the platform?

No. Content, navigation, and layout improvements can be made on the existing platform when it supports the requirements. A rebuild should have a clear reason and a migration plan.

### Can a redesign affect SEO?

Yes. Changes to content, URLs, links, rendering, or indexing can affect search visibility. Inventory important pages, plan changes, test the release, and monitor afterward. Preserving rankings is not guaranteed.

### How much do website redesign services cost?

The cost depends on the review scope, page types, content work, integrations, migration, testing, and support. Request a scoped estimate with exclusions and recurring costs instead of applying another project's price to your site.

### Should I redesign the entire website at once?

That depends on the problem and implementation. A focused release may be appropriate for isolated issues; larger structural changes need coordination across affected pages. Agree a plan that preserves coherent navigation and working journeys.
