A WordPress development agency should help you choose a maintainable implementation, create an editing experience your team can use, and verify the website before handover. When comparing providers, ask how themes, plugins, content, testing, and ongoing support fit your requirements. A polished frontend is useful evidence, but you also need to inspect how the site is managed.

This guide helps UK and US business owners evaluate WordPress proposals and demonstrations. It focuses on platform-specific questions rather than repeating a general agency shortlist. For Shardwise's offering, explore our [WordPress development service](/services/wordpress-development/).

## Start with the publishing tasks your team performs

List who updates the website and which tasks they need to complete. These might include publishing an article, changing a service description, adding a team member, or uploading a case study. Identify where approvals are needed and which information should be reused across pages.

Ask the agency to demonstrate those tasks in a representative environment. Watch how an editor selects a layout, changes content, previews it, and publishes. A homepage screenshot cannot show whether the editing interface is manageable.

For a fictional consultancy, the most important task might be adding a case study and linking it to the correct service. A useful demonstration would use realistic copy and images, show the service selection, and verify the published page. This is an example for evaluation, not a Shardwise client result.

## Understand the proposed theme and editor approach

Ask whether the proposal uses an existing theme, a custom theme, a block theme, a page builder, or a combination. These are implementation choices with different editing and maintenance responsibilities. Request an explanation tied to your content and team rather than assuming one label guarantees better quality.

WordPress's [block editor documentation](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/wordpress-block-editor/) describes creating content using individual blocks. A project can use those tools to create a controlled editing experience, but the actual configuration and training still matter.

Useful questions include:

- Which parts can an editor change without developer assistance?
- Which layouts are reusable, and which are fixed?
- How are shared sections updated consistently?
- What happens when a heading is long or an image has a different shape?
- How are major design changes handled after launch?

Ask for realistic samples and a mobile preview. Content guardrails can help protect a layout, while overly restrictive controls can make routine updates difficult. Agree the balance your team needs.

## Review the plugin and integration responsibilities

Request a list of planned plugins and explain the role of each. Plugins may handle forms, search, commerce, caching, or connections to external services. The important question is whether the chosen combination is supported, understandable, and appropriate for the requirements.

Ask who maintains custom functionality and how changes are tested. A large plugin count alone does not prove a site is poor, and a small count does not establish reliability. Review the actual dependencies, compatibility, and operational responsibilities.

For each integration, identify the system that owns the data, the required access, and the expected behavior when a request fails. A form that shows success without delivering the enquiry is still a failed customer journey. Confirm how the team will detect and investigate that problem.

## Compare proposals using the same scope

Send each provider the same requirements: important page types, content inventory, editing tasks, integrations, migration needs, and launch priorities. Ask for assumptions, exclusions, and acceptance criteria.

## Check relevant WordPress delivery evidence

Ask for examples that match the work you need. A marketing website does not demonstrate a team's experience with a transactional store, membership system, or complex publishing workflow. Ask what the agency actually designed, built, and maintained.

Where appropriate, request an editor demonstration as well as a public URL. Ask about a difficult update, a compatibility issue, and the handover process. Respect confidentiality; an anonymised example can still explain decisions and responsibilities without exposing private systems.

Use our [web and app agency selection guide](/blogs/how-to-choose-web-and-app-development-agency/) for broader questions about scope and delivery. Keep the WordPress evaluation focused on the editing and dependency decisions that affect this platform.

## Ask how updates and staging are handled

WordPress projects need an agreed update process. Identify who reviews core, theme, and plugin changes, where those changes are tested, and what happens if a release causes a problem. WordPress's [updating guidance](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/updating-wordpress/) recommends backing up before updating.

A staging environment can help review changes before applying them to the public site. Agree how it is isolated, which test data is appropriate, and what is deployed. Our [WordPress staging checklist](/blogs/wordpress-staging-site-checklist/) covers deployment scope, customer journeys, and live verification.

For a store, updates must account for new orders and current data. Discuss [WooCommerce maintenance](/services/woocommerce-maintenance/) and use the [checkout testing checklist](/blogs/woocommerce-checkout-testing-checklist/) to review totals, payments, and order records. A generic homepage check is not enough for a commerce release.

## Include accessibility, performance, and search checks

Agree a review of navigation, forms, text alternatives, narrow layouts, and relevant interaction states. Use the [website accessibility checklist](/blogs/website-accessibility-checklist-small-business/) as a starting point, while defining the depth of evaluation required for the project.

For performance, ask which pages and customer tasks are measured and how third-party assets affect the result. Our [Core Web Vitals checklist](/blogs/core-web-vitals-checklist-small-business/) helps distinguish loading, responsiveness, and layout stability. Installing a performance plugin does not replace investigating the actual page behavior.

Clarify what the proposal includes for search: titles, descriptions, content structure, internal links, redirects, and measurement setup. [Google's SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) explains foundational practices. A WordPress SEO plugin can support implementation, but it does not write useful service content or guarantee rankings.

## Agree licenses, accounts, and handover

Make an inventory of hosting, domains, premium themes, plugins, email services, analytics, and custom source files. Ask who controls each account, who pays recurring charges, and what happens if the support relationship ends.

Clarify the licensing and ownership arrangements in the agreement. Some dependencies require ongoing renewals for updates or support; the relevant vendor's terms determine what is included. Avoid assuming that every third-party asset becomes exclusively yours because the website was commissioned.

Collect editing instructions, setup and deployment guidance, the dependency inventory, known issues, and source access for agreed custom work. Use suitable access controls for credentials rather than placing passwords in a shared general document. Ask the provider to demonstrate how your team will perform routine edits after handover.

## Plan support after launch

Agree how defects within the approved scope are handled and how new features are estimated. Record the contact channel, response expectations, update responsibilities, backup process, and incident escalation. A maintenance plan should describe its actual tasks and exclusions.

Use our [website maintenance checklist](/blogs/website-maintenance-checklist-small-business/) to plan recurring reviews of forms, content, backups, and dependencies. Explore [website maintenance](/services/website-maintenance/) if your team needs ongoing help with those responsibilities.

## A practical demonstration checklist

Before selecting a WordPress development agency, ask to review these tasks:

1. Publish representative content using the proposed editor.
2. Update a shared section and confirm where the change appears.
3. Preview long content and a narrow layout.
4. Submit an enquiry and confirm receipt.
5. Explain a plugin update and its staging checks.
6. Identify license renewals and account ownership.
7. Walk through handover and the first support request.

Record answers and unresolved questions against the same requirements for each provider. The goal is a website your customers can use and your team can manage, with clear responsibilities after launch.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose a custom theme or a page builder?

Choose based on design requirements, editing tasks, maintenance capacity, and the provider's proposed approach. Ask for a demonstration and an explanation of dependencies. Neither option is automatically the right choice for every project.

### Can my team update the website after an agency builds it?

Yes, when editing access, training, and suitable layouts are included in the scope. Confirm which tasks your editors can perform and which changes require development support.

### Does the build include WordPress maintenance?

Only if the proposal says so. Agree what happens after the initial support period and who manages updates, backups, incidents, and recurring licenses.

### How much does a WordPress agency project cost?

Cost depends on scope, design, content, integrations, migration, testing, and support. Request an estimate with deliverables, assumptions, and ongoing costs rather than comparing a broad headline price.
