A web and app development agency helps a business plan, design, build, and maintain websites and applications. To choose one, compare how each team defines your scope, demonstrates relevant work, tests customer journeys, and hands over the finished product. A useful proposal connects your business goal to deliverables you can inspect.

This guide is for UK and US business owners comparing development partners. It gives you a shared brief, questions for discovery calls, and a practical way to evaluate proposals. The recommendations apply whether you need a service website, an ecommerce store, a customer portal, or a mobile application.

## Start with the customer task you need to improve

Before discussing frameworks, describe what a customer or employee needs to accomplish. A service business may need visitors to find the right service and submit a qualified enquiry. A retailer may need dependable checkout and inventory integration. A software startup may need users to register, complete one core workflow, and manage a subscription.

Write one primary outcome and the evidence you will use to review it. For example: a completed enquiry reaches the intended inbox with the correct details; a customer can place a test order; or a staff member can process a request through an agreed approval flow. These are acceptance criteria you can demonstrate before release.

Separate launch requirements from later ideas. If reporting, messaging, subscriptions, and a mobile app all appear in the first brief, ask which feature is essential to the first useful release. A smaller coherent scope is easier to compare and verify.

## Decide whether you need a website, web app, or mobile app

These project types have different responsibilities. A website usually presents information and helps visitors take an action. A web application supports interactive workflows such as account management, booking, or a customer portal. A mobile app adds device and platform considerations, release preparation, and ongoing compatibility work.

## Prepare one brief for every shortlisted agency

Comparable proposals start with comparable information. Send the same brief to each team, including these details:

- Your audience, target markets, and main customer task.
- Required pages or user journeys, with priorities and exclusions.
- Existing website, content, designs, or systems that must be retained.
- Integrations such as payment, email, booking, or customer management.
- Who supplies copy, images, translations, and project approvals.
- A budget range, any genuine deadline, and the reason for that deadline.
- Launch acceptance criteria and the support you expect afterward.

For a fictional example, a consultancy could specify five service pages, one enquiry form, editable case studies, and a migration from an existing site. The form requirement should include delivery verification, not just a confirmation message. This example illustrates a brief; it is not a Shardwise client result.

Ask agencies to identify assumptions and unresolved questions. If a quote assumes you will supply final content, record that dependency before comparing it with a quote that includes content work.

## Check relevant delivery evidence

A portfolio is useful when the provider explains its role and the decisions behind the work. Ask to see a project with a similar workflow or constraint. A visually impressive landing page does not show how the same team handles account permissions, a checkout migration, or a native app release.

Useful follow-up questions include:

1. Which parts did your team design and build?
2. What was the most difficult constraint, and how did you handle it?
3. What changed between the first brief and launch?
4. How was the work tested, and what was handed over?
5. Can you show an appropriate live example or a permitted demonstration?

Respect confidentiality. A provider may need to use a demonstration or anonymized explanation rather than sharing private systems. Look for a clear explanation of responsibilities and evidence that can appropriately be shown.

## Compare scope, costs, and change handling

Read the proposal as a list of deliverables. It should identify page types or features, design stages, integrations, testing, launch responsibilities, and exclusions. Ask what happens if you request an additional feature after scope approval.

Compare total responsibilities as well as the initial build price. Hosting, domains, paid plugins, email delivery, app accounts, third-party APIs, and maintenance may introduce recurring costs. Ask who pays each provider and what usage limits matter. Currency and payment arrangements should be explicit for a UK or US engagement.

Request milestones tied to reviewable work: approved navigation, a working core journey, integration tests, and release readiness. A calendar date without input deadlines or review responsibilities is difficult to rely on. Record who approves a change and how its cost and timing are agreed.

This is project planning guidance. Ownership, licensing, and payment arrangements should be documented in the agreement and reviewed appropriately for your situation.

## Ask how testing, accessibility, and SEO are handled

Ask for the test plan that matches your real journeys. An enquiry site needs form delivery checks, keyboard navigation, mobile layouts, and meaningful validation errors. A web app may also need permission checks and integration failure scenarios. A store needs test orders and payment-status verification.

Accessibility review should go beyond a tool score. [W3C's Easy Checks](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/easy-checks/) provides initial checks such as keyboard access and text alternatives, while explaining that a first review does not replace a comprehensive evaluation. Agree the scope and the evidence expected from testing.

For performance, ask which pages and interactions will be measured. [The Web Vitals overview](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) distinguishes user-experience metrics for loading, responsiveness, and visual stability. Our [Core Web Vitals checklist](/blogs/core-web-vitals-checklist-small-business/) can help you turn that discussion into a practical review.

For search visibility, clarify responsibility for titles, descriptions, crawlable content, redirects, sitemap generation, and analytics setup. [Google's helpful-content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) emphasizes useful content for readers. A technical setup supports that work; it does not guarantee rankings or replace a content strategy.

## Agree ownership, access, and handover before launch

Make an account and asset inventory before the final week. Agree who controls the domain, hosting, repository, design files, analytics, and any app-store accounts. Give providers the access they need while keeping business account administration clear.

The handover should include setup instructions, deployment steps, dependencies, environment configuration guidance, known issues, and a walkthrough of the edits your team will make. Store secrets through appropriate account controls rather than placing passwords or private keys in general documentation.

Ask the provider to demonstrate a normal update and explain how another developer could maintain the project. Confirm licensing and ownership terms for custom work and third-party assets. Collect the agreed deliverables while the project context is still fresh.

## Plan support after the first release

Agree the difference between fixing defects within the approved scope and adding new features. Record the support period, contact channel, response expectations, backups, and responsibility for platform updates. A support promise is more useful when it identifies what happens after an incident is reported.

For a website, the [maintenance checklist](/blogs/website-maintenance-checklist-small-business/) helps identify recurring checks. A [website maintenance service](/services/website-maintenance/) can support ongoing work when your team does not have the capacity to perform it. Confirm the actual included tasks before choosing a plan.

## A practical discovery-call checklist

Use these questions to keep your comparison focused:

1. What would you include in the first release, and what would you postpone?
2. Which assumptions affect your estimate?
3. Who will design, develop, test, and approve the work?
4. What evidence will we review at each milestone?
5. How will changes, recurring costs, and delays be handled?
6. Which accounts and files will we control at handover?
7. What support is available after launch?

Record answers against the same criteria for every team. Investigate unanswered questions before signing rather than trying to infer the missing scope from a polished presentation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can one agency build both a website and an app?

Yes, when its delivery team has the relevant skills. Ask how the website, backend, and app share data, who owns each part, and how changes are coordinated. A broad service list alone does not establish experience with your particular project.

### Should I choose the lowest development quote?

Compare quotes only after aligning scope, exclusions, testing, and handover. A lower price can be appropriate for a simpler delivery model, but missing content work or ongoing costs can make the comparison misleading.

### How long does a website or app project take?

Timing depends on scope, content readiness, integrations, review cycles, and release requirements. Ask for milestones and dependencies for your project instead of applying a universal estimate to every build.

### Do I need a separate SEO agency?

That depends on the development team's agreed remit and your search goals. Clarify whether the build includes technical foundations only or also ongoing keyword research, content, and measurement. Assign responsibilities so important work does not fall between teams.
