Bespoke website development means planning and building a website around your business's specific requirements. A useful project brief explains what customers need to do, how content is organised, which systems the website connects to, and how your team will manage it after launch. Those decisions make a tailored build easier to estimate and review.

This guide helps UK business owners prepare that brief before requesting proposals. US teams can use the same planning steps; bespoke and custom commonly describe the same general approach. For a broader overview of delivery options, read our [custom website development services guide](/blogs/custom-website-development-services-guide/).

## 1. Identify the reason for a bespoke build

Start with a specific limitation in the current experience. Perhaps visitors cannot find a service for their industry, editors repeat the same information across many pages, or enquiries reach a shared inbox without enough detail to route them. Describe the consequence and the task that should work better.

For each requirement, consider whether an existing platform feature or supported extension already solves the problem. Custom code is useful when the requirement warrants it, but a bespoke project can still use established libraries and standard services. Ask your development partner to explain which parts need tailoring and which can be reused.

Avoid using a desired technology as the only project goal. “Use a modern framework” is less useful than “our editors must publish a case study without asking a developer to change the layout.” The second statement can guide design, platform selection, and testing.

## 2. Write the customer journeys before the page list

Choose a small set of important tasks and describe each from start to finish. Include the visitor's starting point, the information they need, the action they take, and the result expected by your business.

For a fictional training provider, one journey might be: find a course, compare its dates and entry requirements, submit an enquiry, and have the request reach the appropriate adviser. The brief should identify where course information comes from and how the adviser receives the request. This is a planning example, not a Shardwise client case study.

Write down the less convenient paths too. What should happen if a course has no scheduled dates, a visitor submits an invalid email address, or the receiving system is unavailable? These states affect the interface and integration work.

If the requirements include accounts, permissions, or substantial business logic, discuss whether the project includes a [web application](/services/web-app-development/). That can change the scope beyond an informational website.

## 3. Model the content your team will maintain

A list of pages tells a designer what visitors might see. A content model explains how the information is stored, reused, and edited. Identify repeated entities such as services, case studies, team profiles, locations, products, or resources.

For each entity, list its essential fields and relationships. A case study might have a title, sector, service, summary, images, and a result supported by evidence. Decide whether editors select a related service from a shared list or type a new label each time. Shared references can help prevent inconsistent naming as content grows.

Agree who writes and approves content, how images are supplied, and what happens when an item is archived. Provide realistic sample content before approving page layouts. Short placeholder headings can hide problems with long service names or detailed descriptions.

The editing approach may be a [WordPress implementation](/services/wordpress-development/), another content platform, or a repository-based workflow. The brief should explain your team's editing needs before a platform is selected.

## 4. Map integrations and responsibilities

For every connection to an external system, record the purpose, the data exchanged, and the person who can confirm its requirements. A booking, payment, email, or customer-management integration needs more detail than “connect to our tools.”

Ask your provider to identify missing documentation or access before confirming implementation scope. Use approved test accounts and suitable test data for demonstrations. Share private credentials through appropriate access controls rather than including them in a general project brief.

## 5. Include accessibility and search requirements early

Describe the accessibility review expected from the project and who owns it. Cover important journeys, interaction states, content, and the ongoing publishing process. [W3C's accessibility planning guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/) treats accessibility as work to plan and manage across a project, rather than a single check at the end.

For example, your brief can request review of keyboard navigation, form feedback, text alternatives, and narrow layouts. Our [website accessibility checklist](/blogs/website-accessibility-checklist-small-business/) gives initial checks; those checks alone do not establish comprehensive conformance.

Plan search foundations alongside the content structure. Identify existing URLs worth retaining, page titles and summaries, internal links, and who supplies useful copy. [Google's SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) explains foundational practices for helping people and search engines understand content. A bespoke build still needs that content and ongoing measurement; custom code alone does not guarantee rankings.

For an existing site, include migration responsibilities in the brief. The [website migration SEO checklist](/blogs/website-migration-seo-checklist/) covers URL decisions and release verification. Agree which old pages are retained, redirected, or removed before replacing the site.

## 6. Turn requirements into acceptance checks

An acceptance check describes observable evidence that the agreed work is complete. Make the checks specific enough for a business owner and developer to review together.

- **Enquiry journey:** submit valid details and confirm receipt in the intended destination.
- **Content editing:** publish a representative item and confirm the correct layout and related links.
- **Navigation:** reach key content on a narrow screen and with the agreed keyboard interactions.
- **Integration:** exercise a supported test scenario and confirm the resulting record in the connected system.
- **Migration:** verify important old URLs lead to the intended new pages.
- **Recovery:** demonstrate the agreed recovery process in an appropriate test environment.

Define the supported browsers and devices, the test environment, and who approves the results. Performance review should also name the pages and interactions being measured. Our [Core Web Vitals checklist](/blogs/core-web-vitals-checklist-small-business/) can help structure that discussion.

## 7. Separate launch scope from later improvements

Group your requirements into launch essentials, useful additions, and later ideas. This helps a provider explain an achievable first release and price optional work separately. It also gives your team a clear way to discuss a change without losing track of the original commitment.

Identify dependencies such as approved content, access to existing systems, design feedback, and stakeholder decisions. Ask for milestones tied to reviewable outputs. A proposal should explain assumptions, exclusions, recurring costs, and how changes are approved.

Bespoke website development does not have one universal cost or timeline. Content volume, integrations, migration, editing workflows, and testing all affect the work. Compare estimates against the same brief rather than comparing page counts alone.

## 8. Plan a maintainable handover

Specify the assets and instructions your team needs after release: source access, design files, an account inventory, editing guidance, deployment instructions, known issues, and a support contact. Document which routine tasks your editors can handle and which require development support.

Agree responsibility for updates, backups, renewals, and incident handling. Ask how another developer could understand and maintain the implementation. Ownership and licensing arrangements should be documented in the project agreement rather than assumed from the word bespoke.

For continuing care, explore [website maintenance](/services/website-maintenance/). Agree the actual tasks and support expectations for your chosen architecture.

## A project brief you can copy

Use these headings in a short document and attach relevant examples:

1. Business goal and intended audience.
2. Three priority customer journeys.
3. Content types, sample content, and editing responsibilities.
4. Required integrations and their system owners.
5. Accessibility, search, and migration scope.
6. Acceptance checks and supported environments.
7. Launch essentials, optional features, budget, and dependencies.
8. Handover and ongoing support requirements.

Ask shortlisted teams to identify missing information and explain their assumptions. Our [agency selection guide](/blogs/how-to-choose-web-and-app-development-agency/) provides discovery-call questions for evaluating their answers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is bespoke website development different from custom development?

The terms often describe the same general idea: tailoring a website to specific requirements. Ask what the provider includes, because the label does not define the platform, deliverables, or ownership terms.

### Do I need a complete technical specification before contacting an agency?

No. Start with your business goal, customer journeys, existing systems, and constraints. A discovery phase can develop the detail. Make unresolved requirements visible so a preliminary estimate is not mistaken for a fully defined commitment.

### Can a bespoke website use a CMS?

Yes. Custom page layouts and workflows can be built around a content management system. Choose the editing approach around who publishes the content and the tasks they need to perform.

### Should every feature be custom-built?

No. Supported platform features and integrations may solve parts of the brief effectively. Reserve custom work for requirements that justify it and agree how those parts will be maintained.
